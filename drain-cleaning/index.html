When a Clogged Drain Becomes an Emergency

A clog is an emergency once water cannot leave the building or starts coming back into it. Our emergency drain cleaning line in Maple Shade is open 24/7, and we book same-day visits when the schedule allows. While you wait, stop running water, keep the washer and dishwasher off, and keep children and pets away from any sewage.

Several drains slow at once

A tub gurgling when the toilet flushes usually means the blockage is in the main line, not in one fixture.

Water backing up into a floor drain

Water coming up in a basement floor drain or laundry standpipe is a sign the main is blocked downstream.

Sewage smell with standing water

Dirty water or odor in a tub or shower is a health concern and should be cleared the same day.

A single fixture that will not clear

A kitchen sink or shower that holds water after a plunger is a branch-line clog and can usually wait for a morning slot.

How We Clear a Drain

The method depends on what is blocking the pipe and how far down it sits. We start with the least invasive tool that will do the job and step up only if the line does not run free.

If a line clears but returns within weeks, the clog was a symptom. A camera pass tells us whether the pipe is cracked, sagging or full of roots, and that decides whether cleaning, jetting or a repair is the real fix.

Kitchen Sinks and Grease

Kitchen sink clogs are the most common drain call we hear about, and grease is the usual cause. Cooking fat cools inside the pipe, coats the walls and catches food particles until the opening is the size of a pencil. A cable can punch a hole through it, but jetting is what removes the coating.

A garbage disposal downstream of a clog often hums without moving, so check that before assuming the disposal has failed. If the disposal is the actual problem, our garbage disposal repair page covers it.

Main Line or Branch Line? A Quick Test

Run the closest sink and watch the lowest drain in the house, usually a basement floor drain or a ground-floor tub. If water rises there, the main line is partly blocked and every fixture upstream will eventually back up.

If only one fixture is slow and the rest drain normally, the clog sits in that fixture's branch. That is a smaller job, and it is the case where a quick cable run often ends the problem.

Drains in Older Maple Shade Buildings

Maple Shade added thousands of homes and apartments between 1960 and 1980, according to the township's 2006 master plan. Drain stacks from that era are often cast iron, and cast iron roughens inside as it ages, which gives grease and paper something to catch on. In those buildings we look at the vertical stack and the line out to the street, not only the fixture.

The master plan also records a 1988 sewer-connection moratorium and calls for better storm drainage control. After heavy rain, a backup can come from your own line or from the municipal main, and a camera pass shows which one. If the blockage is past your property line, the township utility is the one to call, and we will say so plainly.

We clear drains in nearby towns as well, including Pennsauken and Cinnaminson, where the housing stock has similar age and the same Route 38 and Route 73 access.

Drain Line Leaks and Slow Recurring Clogs

A drain that clears and then slows again can also be leaking. A cracked drain line lets soil and roots in, and the same crack lets wastewater out. Signs include a damp spot on a basement wall below a bathroom, a persistent smell or a patch of unusually green lawn above the line.

We use a camera to confirm a leak before recommending a repair. If the pipe is damaged, our sewer line repair service covers the fix, and we will explain the options before any work starts.

About insurance

Homeowners policies usually do not pay to clear the clog itself, but some cover water damage that results from a sudden backup. Ask your insurer before the visit and take photos of any damage.

Code referenceIn New Jersey, plumbing work falls under the Uniform Construction Code, and its plumbing subcode follows the National Standard Plumbing Code as adopted in N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15.
