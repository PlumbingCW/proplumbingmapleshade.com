Master Plumber Licensing and Leak Detection in New Jersey

Finding a leak and fixing it are both plumbing work. In New Jersey, the law says who is allowed to do it.

What New Jersey Law Says

New Jersey law requires plumbing work to be performed by a master plumber or an authorized plumbing contractor, under N.J.S.A. 45:14C. The State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers, part of the Division of Consumer Affairs, licenses master plumbers. A journeyman may work, but under the supervision of a master.

That means a person who calls themselves a leak detection specialist and cuts into your pipes is still doing plumbing work. Whether they are allowed depends on the license, not the job title.

Because the diagnosis leads straight to a repair, many homeowners prefer a licensed plumber for both. You hear one plan and one price, and nobody has to hand off responsibility.

Questions to Ask Before You Hire

  • Are you a licensed master plumber or an authorized plumbing contractor in New Jersey?
  • Are you insured, and can you provide proof?
  • How do you find the leak, and will you cut into walls?
  • Will I get a clear price before any repair work starts?
  • Who pulls permits if the repair needs one?

A confident plumber answers these quickly. A hesitant answer on licensing is a reason to keep looking. We do not print license numbers on this site, but you are free to ask for credentials.

What a Leak Detection Visit Looks Like

We start with questions about symptoms, the meter and the age of the plumbing. Then we use a meter test, listening equipment and pressure tests to narrow the location. Thermal imaging can show a warm line in a wall without opening it.

Once we know where the leak is, you get the options and a clear price. See our leak detection page for what each method covers, and burst pipe repair for emergencies.

Red Flags When Hiring

A person who will not state a license status, who insists on cash only or who gives a firm price over the phone for a hidden problem deserves caution. A plumber who explains the diagnosis, shows the evidence and offers to document the work is a better sign.

Check that any estimate describes the work in writing and names who is responsible for permits.

Why This Matters in a Rental

If you rent, the landlord generally decides who does the work. Ask whether the plumber is licensed before approving the visit, since unlicensed work can cause trouble for the owner and the tenant alike.

We can provide a written summary of what we found for the property manager.

How We Work Under the License Rules

We describe the diagnosis in plain words, state the plan and the price before any repair work and tell you when a permit may apply. If a part of a job belongs to another trade, such as drywall or drying, we say so and let you choose that contractor.

That is what the licensing system is meant to produce, a clear line between who does what and who is accountable for it.

Written Records Protect You

Ask for a summary of the work, the date, the address and the scope. If a problem returns, the summary tells the next plumber what was done. If you sell the home, it shows buyers that the work was handled properly.

Keep these papers with the house records. They are worth more than most people realize.

Insurance and Documentation

Ask your plumber for a written summary of what was found and where. Insurers often want it for a claim, and so do landlords. A licensed plumber's report carries more weight than a general contractor's guess.
