Main Water Line Leak Detection: Signs and Next Steps

The line from the street to your house is buried, out of sight and easy to ignore until the bill arrives. Here is how to know whether it is leaking.

By the Maple Shade Pro Plumbing team · Published November 12, 2026 · Plumbing guide for Maple Shade, NJ homeowners

The Short Answer

Key points

  • A meter that moves with every fixture off is the strongest sign of a leak.
  • Closing the main shutoff and reading the meter again tells you whether the leak is inside or in the buried line.
  • Wet lawns, low pressure and rising bills are supporting clues.
  • You generally own the line from the meter or property line to the house.
  • Listening equipment and pressure tests confirm the location before anyone digs.

Signs the Main Line Is Leaking

A spinning meter

The dial moves when every fixture in the house is off.

A wet spot in the yard

Soggy lawn or a puddle without rain or sprinklers.

Low pressure

The whole house loses pressure, not one faucet.

A higher bill

Water use rises with no change in habits.

No single sign proves a leak, but a spinning meter with everything off is the strongest. It means water is leaving the system somewhere between the meter and the fixtures.

A Meter Test You Can Do

1

Turn everything off

Stop all fixtures, the washer, the dishwasher and any ice maker.

2

Read the meter

Note the number or the position of the small leak indicator.

3

Wait

Leave everything off for 30 to 60 minutes.

4

Read it again

Any change means water is moving. If the indicator spins, so is the leak.

To narrow it down, close the main shutoff at the house and read the meter again. If it keeps moving, the leak is between the meter and the shutoff, which is the buried line. If it stops, the leak is inside the house.

How a Plumber Confirms It

We use listening equipment on the pipe and the ground, and pressure tests on the isolated line. Where the line is metal, the sound of escaping water carries along the pipe, and a listening device can point to a spot. Plastic lines are quieter and need more care.

Once the location is known, the plan covers excavation, repair or replacement of that section, and restoration of the surface. A clear price comes before any digging.

Whose Pipe Is It?

The utility owns the main in the street and usually the line up to the meter or the property line. You own the line from there to the house. Rules about where responsibility changes vary, so ask the utility.

A leak at the meter or in the street is a call to the utility. A leak between the meter and the house is yours, and we can find it. See leak detection for the methods, and burst pipe repair if water is escaping fast.

What Excavation Looks Like

If the leak needs digging, we mark the line, locate other utilities and open the smallest area that reaches the pipe. The damaged section is cut out and replaced, and the line is pressure tested before backfill.

Lawns, walks and driveways can be restored afterward, and we describe the plan before work starts. Trenchless options exist for some lines.

Documents and Photos to Keep

Photograph the meter reading, the wet area and the repair. Keep the plumber's summary. If the utility adjusts a bill for a leak, they often want proof of the repair.

Ask the utility about its process, since rules vary.

Preventing Service Line Problems

Keep heavy equipment and new trees away from the line. Know where the main shutoff is and exercise it once a year so it does not seize. Watch the meter once a month for a quiet reading.

If you plan landscaping or a driveway, mark the line first. A call to the state one-call system before digging is required, and it protects you and the utilities.

Signs of a Slow Leak Versus a Break

A slow leak shows as a gradual bill increase or a lawn that stays green and wet. A break shows as a sudden loss of pressure and a geyser or a washed-out patch.

Both need attention. A break needs the main shutoff closed at once, and a slow leak needs a plan.

Why Not Wait?

A leak under a lawn does not hurt anything visible at first, but it wastes water, can wash out soil and can undermine a walk or driveway. A slow leak that becomes a break is an emergency, and it happens at the worst time.
