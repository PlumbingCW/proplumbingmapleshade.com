What the Water Supply Means for Your Fixtures

Mount Laurel Municipal Utilities Authority draws from three wells, 600 to 700 feet deep, in the lower Potomac-Raritan-Magothy aquifer and treats the water at its Elbo Lane facility. Customers are told to assume a blend that can also include water from Willingboro MUA and New Jersey American Water's Delran plant. Because the sources vary, your tap can change from month to month.

The township's 2024 water quality summary lists total hardness between 63 and 138 parts per million as calcium carbonate, with 80 to 100 considered optimal. That range is moderate, so scale is a slow problem and not a daily one. A tankless unit here still benefits from a yearly flush, and a tank from a periodic drain-down.

Homes Built From the Late 1960s On

Mount Laurel was largely rural until the late 1960s, and its biggest population jumps came between the 1960s and the 1990s. Most homes are therefore decades old but not century old. Typical calls include water heaters in their second or third decade, drains slowed by grease and roots and supply lines at the end of their life.

Homes built from the late 1970s into the mid-1990s may contain polybutylene supply pipe, a gray plastic widely used in that period across the United States. Look at exposed pipe near the water heater. If you see it, ask us about repiping before it fails behind a wall.

The South Branch of Pennsauken Creek crosses the western part of the township, so homes there are worth watching in long rain. A failed pump is a problem sooner in low areas, and our sump pump repair page explains what to check.

A Seasonal Plumbing Checklist for Larger Homes

Larger homes mean more fixtures, more outdoor spigots and longer supply runs, which is where seasonal habits pay off. In fall, disconnect hoses and shut off exterior faucets, and check that crawlspace or garage pipes are insulated. In winter, let a faucet drip in a hard freeze and know where the main shutoff is.

In spring, test the sump pump with a bucket of water, look for new stains on basement walls and check the water heater base for dampness. In summer, run every drain, watch for slow ones and note any change in water pressure. A short list beats an emergency, and a note about what you found helps us if you do call.
